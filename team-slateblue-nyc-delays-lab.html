c. What is the advantage of placing the airline names on the y-axis rather than the x-axis in this plot?

Airline names are long character strings that overlap heavily with one another when placed on the x-axis, forcing diagonal or rotated tick labels that are harder to read. Placing them on the y-axis allows each label to sit on its own horizontal line, left-aligned and fully legible without rotation.

e. Advantage and Disadvantage of a lollipop chart over a bar chart:

The thin stem of a lollipop reduces visual clutter when many categories are plotted, our eyes are guided to the endpoint on the dot rather than being drawn to a large filled rectangle, making differences between similar counts easier to perceive.

A lollipop chart does not require a zero baseline as strongly as a bar chart does, which can encourage designers to omit it. However, unlike a bar chart with it has filled area and a truncated axis is immediately misleading. A lollipop’s stem still must start at zero, otherwise the length of the stem no longer encodes the count accurately. Readers are less familiar with lollipop conventions may not notice a missing or shifted baseline.

d. Why is it acceptable for the x-axis of a Cleveland dot plot not to start at zero, whereas the x-axis of a bar chart or lollipop chart always should?

Cleveland dot plot encodes values through the position along a common scale, not through length from a baseline. Readers compare dot positions relative to one another, not relative to the origin like other charts, so a non-zero starting point does not distort the encoded quantities. The axis can legitimately begin near the smallest data value to spread the points apart and make small differences easier to perceive.

Why the unweighted average is a mistake

  • Rates must be weighted by their denominators: Each airport’s on-time percentage is computed from a different number of departures. An unweighted average treats a small airport with 50,000 departures as equally influential as a large airport with 120,000 departures eventhough the larger airport contributes far more observations to the overall rate. The weighted mean corrects this by multiplying each airport’s on-time percentage by its share of total departures, which scales each airport’s contribution proportionally to its share of total departures.

Verifier Task — Time-of-Day Decline with reference to Yahoo News Article

The within-day decline in on-time departure rates observed in Figure 3.6 is a norm in the airline industry. Yahoo Travel (2026) quotes Anton Radchenko, CEO of AirAdvisor, who explains the mechanism directly, “A delay on a 7 a.m. flight does not disappear and follows the aircraft and crew for the rest of the day. By the afternoon, even small disruptions can compound quickly because there is little room left in the system to absorb them.”

Andrew Schmertz, CEO of Hopscotch Air, adds that, “one delay can cause a domino effect” because “planes don’t make money sitting on the ground”, and airlines therefore schedule rotations as tightly as possible, leaving minimal buffer to absorb early disruptions before they cascade into later flights.

This is consistent with the pattern in Figure 3.6, where on-time rates across all three NYC airports are highest in the early morning and fall steadily into the evening. The decline is not a dataset artefact but it reflects real operational mechanics, including the aircraft, crews, and gates are reused across multiple rotations, so a late inbound arrival compresses the turnaround window for the next outbound departure and propagates the delay forward. An airline scheduled heavily into late-day departure slots inherits a structurally lower on-time rate for reasons unrelated to its own scheduling quality, making hour-of-day a confounding variable.

source: Yahoo Travel, 2026

c. Color + Linetype

Mapping airport to both colour and linetype is a deliberate redundancy that serves two distinct protective purposes. The linetype ensures the plot remains interpretable for readers with colour-vision deficiency, who may be unable to distinguish the three coloured lines from one another, while the solid, dashed, and dot-dash patterns remain separable regardless of how colour is perceived.

Also, the linetype mapping preserves legibility when the figure is shown in black and white.

3.7 Schedule Mix by Airline

Data Preparation

schedule_mix <- departures |>
  # Keep only airlines with at least 1,000 departures
  group_by(airline) |>
  filter(n() >= 1000) |>
  ungroup() |>
  # Count departures per airline-hour
  summarise(n = n(), .by = c(airline, hour)) |>
  # Add zero rows for hours with no departures (5 to 23)
  complete(
    airline,
    hour = 5:23,
    fill = list(n = 0)
  ) |>
  # Compute percentage of each airline's departures in each hour

**c. What is the advantage of placing the airline names on the y-axis rather than the x-axis in this plot?**

Airline names are long character strings that overlap heavily with one another when placed on the x-axis, forcing diagonal or rotated tick labels that are harder to read. Placing them on the y-axis allows each label to sit on its own horizontal
line, left-aligned and fully legible without rotation.

**e. Advantage and Disadvantage of a lollipop chart over a bar chart:**

The thin stem of a lollipop reduces visual clutter when many categories are plotted, our eyes are guided to the endpoint on the dot rather than being drawn to a large filled rectangle, making differences between similar counts easier to perceive.

A lollipop chart does not require a zero baseline as strongly as a bar chart
does, which can encourage designers to omit it. However, unlike a bar chart with it has filled area and a truncated axis is immediately misleading. A lollipop's stem still must start at zero, otherwise the length of the stem no longer encodes the count accurately. Readers are less familiar with lollipop conventions may not notice a missing or shifted baseline.

```{r}
#| label: verify-weighted-airport-rate
# Overall on-time percentage computed directly from raw departures
overall_pct_raw <- mean(departures$on_time) * 100

overall_pct_weighted <- ontime_by_airport |>
  summarise(weighted_mean = weighted.mean(on_time_pct, w = n)) |>
  pull(weighted_mean)

# Plain unweighted average of the three airport percentages
overall_pct_unweighted <- mean(ontime_by_airport$on_time_pct)

  round(overall_pct_raw, 4),
  "\n"
)
cat(
  "Departure-weighted mean of airport rates:   ",
  round(overall_pct_weighted, 4),
  "\n"
)
cat(
  "Unweighted mean of airport rates:           ",
  round(overall_pct_unweighted, 4),
  "\n"

  round(overall_pct_unweighted - overall_pct_raw, 4),
  "percentage points\n\n"
)
```
**Why the unweighted average is a mistake**

- Rates must be weighted by their denominators: Each airport's on-time percentage is computed from a different number of departures. An unweighted average treats a small airport with 50,000 departures as equally influential as a large airport with 120,000 departures eventhough the larger airport contributes far more observations to the overall rate. The weighted mean corrects this by multiplying each airport's on-time percentage by its share of total departures, which scales each airport's contribution proportionally to its share of total departures.

- This suggests the raw rank is at least partly confounded by airport mix where an airline flying 80% or more of its routes from a low-punctuality hub will naturally post a lower raw on-time rate than an otherwise equally punctual airline whose departures are concentrated at a more punctual airport.

- **A fair comparison requires controlling for airport exposure:** To assess
  whether the busiest airline is genuinely less punctual than its
  competitors — or merely penalised by its dominant airport — its observed
  on-time rate must be compared against the rate that would be expected
  given the airports and hours it flies. This adjustment is the subject of
  the remaining tasks.

### Verifier Task — Time-of-Day Decline with reference to Yahoo News Article

The within-day decline in on-time departure rates observed in Figure 3.6 is a norm in the airline industry. Yahoo Travel (2026) quotes Anton Radchenko, CEO of AirAdvisor, who explains the mechanism directly, *"A delay on a 7 a.m. flight does not disappear and follows the aircraft and crew for the rest of the day. By the afternoon, even small disruptions can compound quickly because there is little room left in the system to absorb them."* 

Andrew Schmertz, CEO of Hopscotch Air, adds that, *"one delay can cause a domino effect"* because *"planes don't make money sitting on the ground"*, and airlines therefore schedule rotations as tightly as possible, leaving minimal buffer to absorb early disruptions before they cascade into later flights.

This is consistent with the pattern in Figure 3.6, where on-time rates across all three NYC airports are highest in the early morning and fall steadily into
the evening. The decline is not a dataset artefact but it reflects real operational mechanics, including the aircraft, crews, and gates are reused across multiple rotations, so a late inbound arrival compresses the turnaround window for the next outbound departure and propagates the delay forward. An airline scheduled heavily into late-day departure slots inherits a structurally lower on-time rate for reasons unrelated to its own scheduling quality, making hour-of-day a confounding variable.

```{r}
schedule_mix <- departures |>
  # Keep only airlines with at least 1,000 departures
  group_by(airline) |>
```

### a. What does the raw on-time departure ranking reveal, and what does it obscure once airport-hour circumstances are considered?

The raw on-time departure ranking reflects the punctuality that passengers actually experienced, showing that ExpressJet delivered the worst on-time rates and that US Airways delivered among the best. 

What it obscures is whether those differences stem from each airline's own operational choices or merely from the airports and hours it happens to serve. United's ranking shows underperformance but is largely explained by Newark's structurally low baseline, while ExpressJet's last-place ranking appears circumstantial until the airport-hour baseline reveals that it underperforms even the already-disadvantaged schedule it operates. Once airport-hour circumstances are controlled for, the ranking separates into airlines that are genuinely less punctual such as like ExpressJet, which falls well below its own baseline and airlines whose raw rank is more a reflection of where they fly than how well they run their operations.
